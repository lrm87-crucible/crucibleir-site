Surplus equipment disposal process for industrial plants

By Crucible Investment Recovery · September 21, 2026

Most published disposal procedures are written for offices, schools, and labs. Process plants have more to sort out before equipment can leave: cleaning, permits, title, and often proprietary process information. This guide walks through a nine-step process you can adapt for pumps, vessels, exchangers, columns, tanks, and other process equipment. It is general information, not legal, tax, or accounting advice, and rules vary by material and location, so involve your environmental, legal, and finance teams.

1. Declare the asset surplus

Decide who can declare equipment surplus and on what grounds, such as the end of its useful life, a process change, a closure, or being uneconomical to repair. Record the reason and the approver. A written declaration is the starting point for everything that follows, and it starts the clock on how long the asset has been idle.

2. Document what you have

Buyers decide quickly, and they decide on information. For each asset, capture:

  • Asset tag, make, model, serial number, and year
  • Nameplate data and key specifications, such as ratings, materials, and dimensions
  • Photos from several angles, including the nameplate
  • Condition and history, including repairs, inspections, and any modifications
  • Location, and whether it is still installed or already removed and staged
  • Available drawings, manuals, and certificates

3. Check for internal use first

Before you look outside, let other sites see the asset for a fixed window, commonly around 30 days. Internal use usually returns more than a sale because it avoids a new purchase. See our guide to internal redeployment for how to run the window.

4. Clear it to leave the site

This is the step that most often delays a sale. Work through a checklist, and don’t list the equipment until it’s complete:

  • Cleaning and decontamination confirmed and signed off by environmental, health, and safety.
  • Hazardous components and residues identified and handled, such as oils, residual process material, and insulation or lining materials that need special handling.
  • Permits and notifications released or updated where they apply.
  • Title and liens checked, so you can transfer clear ownership.
  • Contract and license restrictions reviewed, including any limits from the original supplier or on proprietary technology.
  • Proprietary information removed, such as process labels, batch records, and configuration data.

5. Estimate what it’s worth and what it costs to move

Set a realistic target before you start. Ask a few dealers or brokers for quotes, look at what comparable equipment has sold for, and compare that with its scrap value. Then subtract the cost of getting it out: disconnecting, rigging, loading, and freight. An asset that’s worth less than it costs to remove is often better scrapped or given away.

6. Choose a sales channel

ChannelBest forSpeedWatch out for
Direct sale to an end userSpecialized equipment with a clear buyerVariesFinding the buyer takes your time
Dealer or brokerMixed lots and hard-to-place itemsModerateFees, and less control over the buyer
Online auction or marketplaceStandard equipment with broad demandOften fastFees, and outcomes depend on bidders
Scrap or recyclerEquipment with little resale valueFastLowest return, so make sure nothing better was possible
DonationLow-value items where reuse mattersVariesConfirm the recipient can take it safely

Crucible doesn’t sell equipment, so it’s no part of this choice. Use whichever channel fits each asset.

7. Agree the terms in writing

  • Whether the sale is as-is and where-is, and what you are and aren’t representing about condition
  • Who removes the equipment, who provides rigging and insurance, and by what date
  • Payment terms, ideally with payment received before the equipment is released
  • Site access, safety induction, and permit-to-work requirements for the buyer’s crew
  • Release of liability and any restrictions on resale or use

Have your legal team review the standard terms once, then reuse them.

8. Supervise removal and handover

Isolate and lock out the equipment, confirm disconnects, and agree a rigging and removal plan before the crew arrives. Have someone from your team present, inspect what leaves the site against what was sold, and get a signed release.

9. Close out the records

  • Remove the asset from your register and record the disposal with your finance team
  • Record the proceeds, or the purchase avoided if it was redeployed
  • Keep the contract, cleaning sign-off, permits, and release together
  • Talk to your tax advisor about how the sale or write-off should be treated

Quick checklist: declared surplus · documented with photos · offered internally · cleaning signed off · permits and title checked · proprietary information removed · value and removal cost estimated · channel chosen · terms agreed in writing · removal supervised · records closed.

Common mistakes

  • Listing before the asset is cleared. It delays the sale and creates risk for both sides.
  • Skipping the internal window. You may sell something another site is about to buy new.
  • Forgetting removal costs. A good price can disappear once rigging and freight are counted.
  • Letting timelines drift. Every month an asset sits, it keeps costing you. The holding cost calculator puts a number on that.
  • Not recording the outcome. Without proceeds and purchases avoided, you can’t show what the program is worth.
