Investment recovery and sustainability reporting: what to measure

By Crucible Investment Recovery · Updated October 10, 2026

Most investment recovery programs are justified on cash. They also produce one of the few circular-economy stories an industrial company can tell with real operating data behind it — and most teams never report it, because nobody wrote down the numbers while the work was happening.

This guide covers what is worth measuring, how to keep the figures defensible, and the one reporting mistake that will get your numbers challenged.

Why investment recovery belongs in a sustainability report

When a plant redeploys an idle heat exchanger to a sister site, three things happen that a sustainability team cares about. A new unit is not manufactured. An existing unit does not become scrap. And the embodied carbon already spent on making that asset keeps doing useful work instead of being written off.

The Investment Recovery Association makes the same connection, describing investment recovery as contributing directly to corporate sustainability goals rather than being purely a financial exercise. The difficulty is almost never the argument. It is that the data lives in people’s heads and in emails, so by the time the report is being written there is nothing to count.

The four metrics worth tracking

These are the ones that survive scrutiny, because each traces back to a specific asset and a specific decision.

  1. Assets redeployed, and their replacement value. The count plus what it would have cost to buy those units new. This is your strongest figure: it is a real avoided purchase, not an estimate.
  2. Assets resold rather than scrapped. By count and by weight. Resale keeps a working asset in service somewhere; scrapping recovers material value only.
  3. Material diverted from scrap or landfill. In tonnes, split by stream where you can — carbon steel, stainless, copper, electrical, other. Waste-diversion rates are a standard reporting line and this feeds it directly.
  4. Avoided purchases, in currency. The finance number and the sustainability number are the same number here, which is unusual and useful. It means the operations team and the sustainability team can cite one figure without reconciling two methodologies.

The reportable statements here are specific and checkable: 80% of assets leaving service were kept in use rather than scrapped; 99 tonnes of equipment were diverted from the scrap stream; $410,000 of new manufacturing was avoided. None of that requires a carbon model to state.

The mistake to avoid

Do not subtract avoided emissions from your reported footprint. Avoided emissions — sometimes called Scope 4 — sit outside Scope 1, 2 and 3 accounting. They describe emissions that did not happen somewhere else, not a reduction in emissions your company actually produced. WBCSD’s 2025 guidance on avoided emissions continues to advise against netting them against a GHG inventory.

Report them alongside your inventory, clearly labelled, with the method stated. A sustainability lead will accept a well-documented avoided-emissions figure presented separately. They will reject one that has been quietly subtracted from Scope 3, and the challenge will land on whoever supplied the number.

If you want to go further than mass and currency and estimate avoided embodied carbon, state the emission factor you used and its source, and present it as an estimate. A transparent estimate with a stated method is defensible. A precise-looking number with no visible method is not.

Keeping the numbers defensible

  • Record at the point of decision, not at year end. The replacement value of a redeployed asset is knowable the week it moves and a guess six months later.
  • Tie every figure to an asset record. If an auditor or a sustainability lead asks which units make up 99 tonnes, you should be able to list them.
  • Capture the disposal route for every asset. Redeployed, sold, scrapped, donated, written off. Diversion rates are impossible to compute if the outcome field is blank.
  • Agree who claims the saving. Decide in advance whether the releasing site, the receiving site, or the corporate program books an avoided purchase — otherwise it gets counted twice, which is the fastest way to lose the whole figure.
  • Keep weights. Nobody wants to weigh a vessel after the fact. Nameplate or engineering mass recorded once is enough.

Who to bring into the conversation

Investment recovery programs are usually justified to operations or finance. The sustainability team is often unaware the data exists, and they are frequently looking for exactly this kind of operating evidence to replace estimates in their reporting. That makes them a useful internal ally when a recovery program needs budget or attention — and it is often the shortest route to getting a program resourced.
