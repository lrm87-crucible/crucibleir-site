What is investment recovery? A plain-English guide

By Crucible Investment Recovery · September 21, 2026

Investment recovery is the work of getting the most value from assets a company no longer needs. Instead of letting idle equipment sit, or sending it to scrap by default, an investment recovery team decides whether it can be reused elsewhere in the company, sold, donated, or recycled. It also makes sure that happens safely and is recorded properly.

Investment recovery, surplus asset management, and asset recovery

In industrial companies, “investment recovery” (often shortened to IR), “surplus asset management,” and “asset disposition” mostly describe the same job. “Asset recovery” is sometimes used that way too, but it also describes unrelated work such as tracing financial assets or recovering collateral. Searching for it returns a confusing mix. If you’re looking for material about idle equipment, “investment recovery” and “surplus asset management” are the more reliable terms.

What counts as an asset

Anything the business owns and no longer uses can be in scope. In a plant that usually means process equipment such as pumps, motors, vessels, heat exchangers, columns and tanks, along with spare parts, instruments, and whole production lines or buildings after a closure. Some teams also handle scrap, byproducts, and other material streams.

What an investment recovery team does

  1. Finds the surplus. It builds and maintains one list of what is idle across every site.
  2. Evaluates it. It checks condition, documentation, and whether anyone in the company could use it.
  3. Decides what happens next. Reuse, sell, donate, recycle, or dispose, based on value and cost.
  4. Clears it to leave. It confirms cleaning, permits, title, and approvals are complete.
  5. Carries it out. It transfers the asset internally or manages the sale and removal.
  6. Records the result. It captures proceeds, purchases avoided, and updates the asset register.

The order of options

Most programs work down a list, from the option that usually returns the most value to the one that returns the least:

  1. Redeploy the asset to another site or unit in the same company.
  2. Sell it whole, as it is, to an end user, dealer, or through a marketplace.
  3. Sell it for parts or components.
  4. Donate it where that makes sense.
  5. Recycle or scrap it for its material value.
  6. Dispose of it as a last resort.

The order isn’t a rule. Transfer costs, condition, timing, and compliance can change the answer for a given asset. But asking “can we use this ourselves?” first is the habit that separates programs that recover value from ones that only clear space. Who is involved

Investment recovery touches several teams. Operations knows the equipment. Finance owns the fixed-asset records. Procurement often knows who is about to buy something. Environmental, health, and safety confirms the asset is clean and safe to move, and legal or tax weighs in on title, contracts, and how a sale is treated. Some companies have a dedicated team, some assign the job to one person, and some use outside brokers or auction houses for part of it. Many mix these.

Why assets get stuck

  • No shared list. Each site tracks its own idle equipment, so nobody sees the whole picture.
  • Unclear decisions. Nobody is sure who can approve a transfer or a sale.
  • Compliance uncertainty. Cleaning, permits, and title questions delay the listing, and the asset keeps costing money while it waits.

How to tell whether the program is working

  • Number of idle assets and their book value
  • Average days from “idle” to a decision
  • Proceeds compared with book value
  • New purchases avoided through redeployment
  • What holding the remaining assets costs each year (try the holding cost calculator)

Getting started: the first 90 days

  1. Weeks 1 to 2: Build one list of idle assets across every site.
  2. Weeks 3 to 4: Decide who can approve a transfer, a sale, and a scrap.
  3. Weeks 5 to 6: Set a redeployment window, commonly around 30 days, before anything is offered outside the company.
  4. Weeks 7 to 8: Write the compliance checklist an asset must complete before it can be listed.
  5. Weeks 9 to 12: Run your first batch through the process and report what you recovered and avoided.

You don’t need software to start. A shared spreadsheet is fine for the first list. Software starts to matter when several sites, approvals, and compliance steps make the spreadsheet hard to trust.

Common questions

Is investment recovery the same as asset recovery?

For idle equipment, usually yes, but “asset recovery” is also used for unrelated financial and collateral work. “Investment recovery” is the clearer term.

Does investment recovery only mean selling things?

No. Reusing an asset inside the company often returns more than selling it, and some assets are best donated or recycled.

Who should own it?

It works best with a named owner and clear decision rights, whether that’s a small central team or one person supported by the sites.
